## Intro: Coffee & the Chapter's Frame (1200–1450)

- **Coffee** originated in **Ethiopia** (Northeast Africa), discovered probably before the 15th century.
- Two origin stories: (1) a **Sufi mystic from Morocco** in Ethiopia saw lively birds eating berries, tried them, gained vitality, shared with Muslims; (2) an **exiled Arab healer** found boiled berries made a drink — brought the "miracle drug" to the Red Sea port of **Mocha** (hence "mocha").
- Coffee spread via **Muslim piety + Arab trade networks** (kept believers awake in prayer) → Mecca, Arab world, South Asia (some conservatives tried and failed to ban it). **Europeans learned of coffee + the coffeehouse only in the 16th century.**
- Chapter frame: region touched by Mongol expansion, shaped by **decline of Arab political institutions** (Middle East) and **religious change and resistance** (Southern Asia). The **continued spread of Islam** + diversity of regional responses set the tone.

## Before 1200 — How the Abbasids Got Here
- Abbasids seized authority violently in **749**, then controlled the heartlands + Central Asia, North Africa, and **Spain**.
- **First loss = most distant:** **Abd al-Rahman**, a surviving Umayyad clansman, fled to Iberia and founded a kingdom — **al-Andalus/Cordoba**, the foremost example of cooperation between Muslims, Jews, and Christians (e.g., the **Great Mosque at Cordoba**, horseshoe arches).
- Berber North Africa broke free within decades; eastern regions (Sind, Afghanistan) became near-independent kingdoms.
- Islam spread mainly by **peaceful, voluntary conversion** — traders, towns; "people of the book" (Jews, Christians, Hindus, Buddhists) worshiped openly and often prospered.
- Muslim civilization's genius = **borrowing then innovating** from Greece, Rome, Byzantium, Persia, Egypt, India, China.

## 5.1 The Abbasid Paradox: Renaissance in a Failing Empire

**Internal collapse (name these causes specifically):**
- **Succession struggles** + rival **mercenary SLAVE armies**: in the **9th century** slave troops **murdered a caliph and installed one of his sons**; over the following decades **4 more caliphs were assassinated or poisoned** by mercenaries → slave-army leaders became the real power behind the throne; also catalyzed **urban food riots**.
- Civil violence drained the treasury; caliphs built **costly new capitals near Baghdad** (palaces, mosques, public works) → **spiraling taxation**, pillaging by local officials → villages destroyed/abandoned in the richest provinces.
- **Tigris-Euphrates irrigation works fell into disrepair or collapsed entirely** → flood, famine, flight to wilderness/other kingdoms; bandit gangs & vagabonds.
- **Shi'a sects instigated peasant uprisings** — aiming not just to correct abuses but to **destroy the Sunni Abbasid regime**.
- Outer territories lost; even **Egypt and Syria** broke away.

**The succession of overlords (memorize this chain):**
- **945 — Buyids of Persia** capture Baghdad; caliphs become **figureheads**; Buyid leaders take the title **sultan** ("victorious" in Arabic). Buyids were **Shi'a**-influenced and promoted Shi'a officials.
- **1055 — Seljuk Turks** (nomadic invaders from Central Asia **via Persia**, **staunch Sunnis**) overthrow the Buyids; rule in the caliphs' name for two centuries. Seljuks **purged Shi'a officials**, restored political initiative, ended the Shi'a Egyptian (Fatimid) threat, **humbled the Byzantines** — opening **Anatolia/Asia Minor** to Turkic settlement = **foundation of the future Ottoman Empire**.

**5.1.1 The Crusades (a "fairly minor episode" for the Middle East):**
- First Crusade **1096–1099** (Muslim divisions + surprise = most successful); **Jerusalem taken June 1099**, Muslim & Jewish inhabitants **massacred**. Christians held coastal Holy Land ~2 centuries; **8 Crusades** total.
- **Saladin (Salah-ud-Din)** united Muslims in the last decades of the 1100s and reconquered most crusader outposts; died **1193**. **Fall of Acre, 1291** = last crusader kingdom gone.
- **Impact bigger on Europe than the Muslim world:** Europeans intensified borrowing — **damascene swords** (named for Damascus), Muslim **fortification/castle techniques** (Normandy/England under **William the Conqueror**), **Richard the Lionheart preferred Muslim physicians**; recovered **Greek learning** via Muslims/Jews in Spain, Sicily, Egypt; adopted **Arabic (really Indian) numerals + decimal system**; Muslim goods & words (fustian, taffeta, muslin, damask); **chess, chivalric ideals, troubadour ballads** (traced to crusader contacts); foods: **dates, coffee, yogurt**. Exchange was largely **one-way** — Muslims showed little interest in Western learning. **Italian merchant communities** did more for exchange than all the knights.

**5.1.2 Women — the harem and the veil (twin emblems of subjugation):**
- **The harem was an Abbasid court creation**; wives + concubines confined to the forbidden quarters of the imperial palace.
- Many concubines were **slaves who could win freedom and power by bearing healthy sons**.
- **One 10th-century caliph reputedly kept 11,000 eunuchs; another 4,000 slave concubines.**
- Slaves came from **Balkans, Central Asia, Sudanic Africa**; prized for beauty AND intelligence; some of the empire's best-educated people. **Slave women had more personal liberty than freeborn wives** (could go to market; didn't have to veil).
- Freeborn women **married at puberty (legally age 9)**; rich women had almost no careers beyond the home; lower-class women farmed, wove rugs/clothing, raised silkworms. Elite wives/concubines plotted with eunuchs/advisors for their sons' succession. By the Abbasid end, women's freedom was **severely curtailed** vs. early Islam.

**5.1.3 The End of the Caliphate:**
- Mongols raided in the **1220s**, smashed the Turkic-Persian kingdoms east of Baghdad; **Hulegu** (Chinggis's grandson) renewed the assault in the **1250s**.
- **1258 — Mongols capture and sack Baghdad; the 37th and final Abbasid caliph is put to death.**
- Mongols pushed west until **defeated by the Mamluks** (Turkic-speaking slave warriors ruling Egypt — same victory as Ain Jalut 1260).
- **Baghdad never recovered**; **1401 — captured and pillaged AGAIN by Timur (Tamerlane)** → shrank from a great world city to a provincial backwater, supplanted by **Cairo**, then **Istanbul**.

## 5.2 An Age of Learning and Artistic Refinement

**Economy/society:** Professional classes expanded (doctors, scholars, legal/religious experts). Muslim, **Jewish**, and some Christian entrepreneurs made fortunes supplying cities (grain, barley, cotton/wool textiles, gems, citrus, sugar cane). Long-distance trade: Middle East ↔ Mediterranean Europe, coastal India ↔ SE Asia, caravans to China.

**5.2.1 The Arts:**
- Mosques/palaces bigger and more ornate; **Cordoba** in Spain = architectural treasure. Persian **rugs and tapestries** in demand from Europe to China; fine **bronzes and ceramics**.
- **LANGUAGE SHIFT (classic quiz point):** with Persian wives, concubines, advisors, bureaucrats and (after mid-10th c.) Persian caliphs dominant, **Persian replaced Arabic as the primary written language at the Abbasid court**. **Arabic remained the language of religion, law, and the natural sciences.** Persian = language of "high culture," literature, administration, scholarship — written in a **modified Arabic script**.
- Key authors:
  - **Omar Khayyam** (1038–1123, scientist AND poet) — the ***Rubaiyat*** ("A jug of wine, a loaf of bread, and Thou"); really about finding meaning/union with the divine.
  - **Firdawsi** (d. c. 1020) — the ***Shah-Nama*** (**"Book of Kings"**), late 10th–early 11th c. epic; history of Persia from creation to the Islamic conquests; grand "musical" Persian; read aloud to music; brilliantly illustrated manuscripts = premier Islamic art.
  - **Sa'di** — great poet blending the mystical with the everyday ("seize the skirts of those who know the way").

**5.2.2 The Sciences:**
- For several centuries Islamic civilization **outstripped all others** in scientific discovery.
- **Algebra & geometry**: major corrections to Greek theories; advances in **trigonometry — sine, cosine, tangent**.
- **Chemistry**: invented the **objective experiment**; **al-Razi's classification of material substances into animal, vegetable, mineral**.
- **Al-Biruni** (973–1050) — calculated the **specific weight of 18 major minerals** in the 11th century.
- Astronomy: instruments from **scholar + artisan cooperation**; astronomical tables/star maps in demand as far as **Europe and China**.
- Medicine: **Cairo had some of the world's best hospitals**; doctors/pharmacists needed a **regular course of study + formal examination to practice**; work on **optics and bladder ailments**.
- **Technology transfer from China:** papermaking, silk-weaving, ceramic firing. **Muslim maps** copied by geographers from **Portugal to Poland**. (Mechanical engineering: Ibn-al-Razzaz **Al-Jazari**, 1136–1206, *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, c. 1200.)

**5.2.3 Scholars, Mystics, and Renewed Expansion:**
- **Two opposing trends:** (1) **mystical resurgence** = new vibrancy; (2) **orthodox ulama** increasingly hostile to non-Islamic ideas and **scientific thinking** — intensified by the **Crusades** (Greek learning associated with Christian Europe). Ulama worried Greek-style questioning would undermine the **absolute authority of the Qur'an** (final, perfect, complete revelation).
- **al-Ghazali** (1058–1111): greatest Islamic theologian; **struggled to fuse Greek and Qur'anic traditions**; **not fully accepted by the ulama**.
- **Sufis** — name from their **woolen robes**; not a formal organized movement; identified from the **8th century**; reaction against the **impersonal, abstract divinity** of ulama teaching. Practices: asceticism, meditation, songs, drugs, **ecstatic dancing (whirling dervishes)**. Some famous as **healers/miracle-workers**; others **led militant bands** to spread Islam. Most insisted Allah was distinct from humans; some teachings had Allah permeating the universe. Sufism = **central factor in Islam's continuing expansion** in the late Abbasid era.

## 5.3 The Spread of Islam in South Asia

**Why the Muslims were different from every previous invader:**
- Earlier incomers (nomads, refugees) were always **absorbed** — India's material culture was higher, its civilizations flexible.
- Muslims = the first large-scale influx from an **equally sophisticated outside civilization**, with a religion that was the **opposite** of Hinduism:
  - **Islam:** doctrinaire, **proselytizing**, **exclusive worship of one transcendent god**, **egalitarian** (all believers equal before God), mobile community of believers.
  - **Hinduism:** validated the **caste hierarchy**, inborn differences, many deities, closed/compartmentalized society.
- Still: pattern = **accommodation + occasional conflict**, not endless war. Muslims **employed large numbers of Hindus** to govern; Hindu-Muslim **mystics** sought common ground; but tensions periodically erupted in riots/wars.

**Timeline (from the margins — quiz gold):**
- **Muhammad ibn Qasim** — Arab general, **conquered Sind + Indus valley** (8th c.; declared it part of the Umayyad dynasty).
- Long pause: disputes between Arabs in Sind and the Umayyad, then Abbasid, caliphs weakened the foothold; Hindus recovered the Indus valley.
- **Mahmud of Ghazni** (971–1030) — **third ruler of a Turkish SLAVE dynasty that seized power in Afghanistan** (late 10th c.); mounted a **series of expeditions** in the **first decades of the 11th century**, raiding NW India again and again, defeating confederations of Hindu princes, looting ever-richer **temples** (sacked one of the wealthiest Hindu temples in Northern India) → **gave Muslims a reputation for intolerance and aggression**.
- Late 12th c.: raids → sustained conquest. **Muhammad of Ghur** (1173–1206) — Persian-extraction commander from a small Afghan mountain kingdom; won the **Indus valley, Sind, and much of North-Central India**; lieutenants pushed down the **Gangetic plain** to Bengal.
- **Qutb-ud-din Aibak** — Ghur's lieutenant; established the kingdom with capital at **Delhi** (on the **Jumna River**, Gangetic plain); **proclaimed himself sultan (r. 1206–1210)**.
- **Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526)** — 300 years of dynasties (Persian, Afghan, Turkic), "princes of the heartland," fought each other + Mongols/Turkic invaders + Hindu princes. **Delhi sultanate successfully resisted Mongol attacks in the 1290s.**

**5.4.1 Patterns of Conversion (very quiz-friendly):**
- Largest Muslim communities: **Bengal** (east) and the **northwestern Indus valley** (entry point of invaders).
- **Surprisingly FEW converts in the Indo-Gangetic political heartland** → evidence that **forced conversion was of limited importance**.
- Conversions clustered where **Buddhism had survived** (W. India, Bengal): esoteric rituals + corrupt practices had debased Buddhism and monastic morale; **Muslim raids on Buddhist temples/monasteries** (vulnerable, lucrative targets) finished it off → disorganized Buddhists converted more readily.
- **Sufis were the key missionaries**: shared style and message with Indian gurus/wandering ascetics; believed to have **magical/healing powers**; mosques + schools became regional power centers; organized devotee **militias** vs. bandits/rival princes; **oversaw forest clearing for farming/settlement**; **welcomed low-caste and outcaste Hindus**; their **tombs became veneration sites for Muslims AND Hindus/Buddhists**.
- **Untouchables, low-caste Hindus, tribal animists** attracted by Islam's **egalitarian social arrangements**; **GROUP conversions were essential** (individual converts would be shunned by the caste system).
- Some converted to **escape the head tax (jizya) on unbelievers**.
- Migration swelled the community too: in the **13th–14th centuries Turkic, Persian, and Afghan peoples fled to India from the Mongol and Timurid conquests**.

**5.4.2 Patterns of Accommodation (Indianization of Islam):**
- **al-Biruni quote** on Hindu haughtiness: "no country but theirs, no nation like theirs… haughty, foolishly vain, self-conceited and stolid."
- Separate **ranis/quarters** everywhere Muslim communities settled; genuine friendships between high-caste Hindus and Muslims rare; liaisons restricted.
- Hindus staffed Muslim **bureaucracies and armies**; Muslim princes adopted **Hindu-inspired regal styles** against the Qur'an: **claims of divine descent**, coins with **Hindu images (Nandi, Shiva's bull)**.
- Muslim society became **divided along caste lines** (Arabs, then Turks, then Persians in the hierarchy; "clean" artisan/merchant groups next); adopted **Indian foods, dress, chewing pan (betel nut + limestone)**; married women at earlier (Hindu) ages; banned **widow remarriage**; some high-caste Muslims even practiced **sati** (burning widows on husbands' funeral pyres).
- **Why conversions were fewer than expected:** Islam's egalitarianism was blunted by adopting caste; also Hindu resistance. Muslim communities survived as a large, distinct minority.

**5.4.3 Islamic Challenge and Hindu Revival:**
- Hindus couldn't absorb Islam and realized it was an actively proselytizing threat → **devotional revival: the bhakti cults**.
- **Bhakti:** emotional bond between devotee and god/goddess; chants/dances → **ecstasy removes all past sins and renders caste distinctions meaningless**; main deities: **Shiva, Vishnu (esp. as Krishna, the goat-herder), and the goddess Kali**.
- Open to women: **Mira Bai (1498–1547)**, celebrated religious poet.
- **Kabir (1440–1518)** — **Muslim mystic** who **played down ritual differences between Hinduism and Islam** ("I am neither in temple nor in mosque…"); won few converts from either side and was **repudiated by orthodox guardians of both faiths**.
- Bhakti songs/poems in **vernacular languages (Bengali, Marathi, Tamil)** → accessible to common people, major popular-culture force. **Bhakti probably stemmed the flow of low-caste converts to Islam.**
- Muslim ulama responded in kind: stressed teachings that **separated Islam from Hinduism**, promoted unity within the Indian Muslim community and ties to the Middle Eastern heartland.
- **Bottom line by 1450 (name it):** South Asia remained **one of the least converted and least integrated of all areas Islam reached** — large Muslim community + political control, but Hinduism unbroken.

**5.4.4 Indian Influences ON Islamic Civilization:**
- **Indian (Hindu) numerals** → passed to **Arab thinkers**, then to **Europe** → why we call them "**Arabic numerals**" though they **originated in India**. Central to **two scientific revolutions** (Middle East, then Europe from the 16th century).
- Indian works on **algebra, geometry, astronomy** translated in Baghdad; **Indian astronomical instruments** copied by Arab astronomers; Indian medical treatises; **Indian doctors** ran Baghdad's hospitals (crusaders envied them) and **cured rulers Greek physicians had given up on**; Indian music/melodies; **chess** became a favorite of royalty and townspeople.

## 5.5 The Spread of Islam to Southeast Asia

**Background — the trading middleman zone:**
- Island SE Asia = meeting point of the **Chinese trading sphere** and the **Indian Ocean trading zone**; ports on the **Malay peninsula, East Sumatra, later North Java** transferred goods between **Chinese and Arab/Indian ships**.
- Exports: **aromatic woods (Borneo, Sumatra rainforests), spices — cloves, nutmeg, mace** from the far Indonesian archipelago.
- From the **8th century**, India's coastal trade increasingly controlled by Muslims from **Gujarat (western India)** and South India → Islamic culture filtered in.

**The gatekeeper falls (KEY SEQUENCE):**
- **Shrivijaya** — trading empire centered on the **Strait of Malacca** (between Malaya and NE Sumatra); rulers/officials were **devout Buddhists** → **little incentive for local traders to convert** while it stood.
- **13th century: Shrivijaya's collapse** opened the way for widespread conversion.
- **First conversions: late 13th century, small port towns on the northern coast of Sumatra** (also the textbook's margin date: "1290s — Beginning of the spread of Islam in Southeast Asia").
- Spread path (memorize): **Sumatra ports → across the Strait of Malacca to Malaya → the great trading city of MALACCA (tip of the Malayan peninsula, replaced Shrivijaya) → East Sumatra → DEMAK (most powerful trading state on the north coast of Java) → other Javanese ports → eventually the whole island → eastern archipelago/Spice Islands → MINDANAO in the southern Philippines.**
- **Why port cities converted (quiz-worthy logic):** coastal areas were most receptive; once one city in a trading cluster converted, others followed to **enhance trade ties and provide a common basis in Muslim law** to regulate deals; conversion linked them culturally to India, the Middle East, the Mediterranean.
- Slow progress in **Central Java** (Hindu-Buddhist interior kingdoms). But because earlier Hindu/Buddhist conversion had been **confined to ruling elites**, the mass of peasants was open to Sufi-led **mass conversion** — same on mainland coasts.

**5.5.2 Sufi Mystics and the Nature of SE Asian Islam (accommodation again):**
- Islam there was **infused with mystical strains and pre-Islamic (animist/Hindu/Buddhist) elements**; Sufis allowed converts to keep them.
- **Pre-Islamic customary law stayed dominant** for social interaction; Islamic law confined to specific agreements/exchanges.
- **Women kept a much stronger position** than in the Middle East/India: **small-scale female buyers and sellers dominated local/regional markets**; in **western Sumatra (Minangkabau area), lineage and inheritance stayed MATRILINEAL** (traced through the female line) even after conversion.
- Pre-Muslim cultural staples kept and refined: the **Javanese shadow-puppet plays (wayang) based on the Indian epics** became MORE central after Islamization; **krises** (magic forged daggers) stayed treasured by rulers before and after conversion.
- Textbook's general point (Thinking Historically): Islam spread **overwhelmingly by peaceful trade + Sufi preaching**, not force; converts kept nature-spirits/ancestors (redefined as **emissaries to Allah**); purification/revival movements periodically pushed back when accommodation went too far.

## Global Connections — Islam as a Bridge Between Worlds

- Abbasid centuries **solidified Islam's central position** in global history: the **go-between** for the older civilizations; enriched nomadic peoples (Turks, Mongols, Berbers, Saharan camel-herders).
- Original contributions in arts, sciences, literature, religion, philosophy "would affect the development of human societies in major ways for centuries to come."
- **BUT — the fatal weaknesses (setting up later chapters):** Muslim **political divisions** + the caliphate's collapse left openings **Europeans would exploit**; the **growing orthodoxy/intolerance of the ulama** and Muslim self-sufficiency ("the Islamic world contained all requirements for civilized life") made Muslims **less receptive to outside innovation** — exactly when **Christian Europe entered an age of curiosity, experimentation, and exploration**.
