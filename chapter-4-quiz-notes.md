## Intro: The Mongol Invasions of Japan

- After toppling the Song, **Kubilai Khan** launched invasions of **Korea, Japan, and Vietnam** *before* Chinese resistance even ended in 1279.
- **Korea** capitulated first → became a **vassal**; provided ships, sailors, and soldiers (Mongols lacked both ships and sailors).
- **1274 — First invasion of Japan (Kyushu):**
  - Kubilai sent a letter demanding vassalage to **Hojo Tokimune** (most powerful leader in divided Japan).
  - Tokimune **refused an audience AND had the Mongol emissaries beheaded**.
  - Invaders = tens of thousands, mostly **Mongols + Koreans + Chinese** (numbers inflated by chroniclers).
  - Samurai first fought with traditional **one-on-one duels** → massed Mongol units overwhelmed them.
  - Mongols used **poison arrows, fire arrows, and ceramic projectiles filled with GUNPOWDER** (Chinese invention; Mongols were the first to use it extensively in conquest).
  - Samurai's legendary **steel swords couldn't penetrate Mongol thick leather armor**.
- **The typhoons (kamikaze):**
  - A **ferocious typhoon** destroyed most of the Mongol fleet as it regrouped → thousands drowned; samurai boarded ships and killed survivors. Kubilai forced to end campaign.
  - **1281 — Second, even larger expedition:** Japanese had anticipated it, **built forts along Kyushu's coast, including a low wall on the northern end**. Samurai defended the walls, inflicted heavy casualties; a **second typhoon** destroyed the fleet → Kubilai gave up.
- Aftermath:
  - Samurai smiths **refashioned/refined swords** (admired for centuries after).
  - **Political watershed:** lords banded together against a foreign foe — brief sense of **national purpose** uniting the warrior elite (divisions returned after the danger passed).
  - Tokimune had relied on a **Buddhist monk** during the ordeal → became a major patron of **Zen Buddhism** and the **Bushido** warrior tradition.
- Impact pattern: Mongol impact on East Asia varied by the **balance between conquest and resistance** (China conquered; Japan/Vietnam resisted) → new divisions, esp. between Chinese and Japanese experiences.

## 4.1 The Mongol Era in Chinese History (Yuan dynasty, 1271–1368)

**Preserving Mongol separateness:**
- **Mongolian language** used for records and correspondence at upper levels of government.
- Mongol religious ceremonies/customs retained; a **tent encampment** set up in the imperial city.
- Kubilai **REFUSED to restore the civil service exams** (discontinued earlier by the **Jin** dynasty) despite Confucian advisors' entreaties.
- **Yuan social hierarchy (4 tiers):** 1) **Mongols** at top → 2) **Muslims** and other non-Chinese allies (2nd highest) → 3) **ethnic/north Chinese** → 4) **southern Chinese** (minority peoples of the south) lowest.
- Ethnic Chinese ran the bureaucracy at **regional and local levels only**; could exercise power at top only as advisors. An enlarged, more intrusive **censors bureau** of Mongol functionaries scrutinized officials.

**Yet Kubilai also Sinicized:**
- Capital at **Dadu (present-day Beijing)**, built on the site of earlier Chinese cities; introduced **Chinese rituals and classical music** at court; abandoned the tent for an **imperial palace**; major **patron of Chinese arts**.

**Gender roles:**
- **Mongol women stayed independent** — rode to the hunt (with husbands or leading their own parties); daughter of one of Kubilai's cousins **went to war and refused to marry until a suitor could beat her in a wrestling match**.
- **Chabi (d. c. 1281)** — Kubilai's wife, most influential:
  - Key confidant on **political and diplomatic matters**; promoted **Buddhist interests** in government.
  - Convinced Kubilai that harsh treatment of defeated **Song imperial family survivors** would anger North China.
  - Countered the schemes of Kubilai's **ambitious brother**; advised on defusing **scholar-gentry hostility** and peasant distrust.
  - **Stopped the plan to turn cultivated lands near the capital into pasture** for Mongol ponies.
- Mongol era too brief to reverse the long decline of Chinese women's status — **Neo-Confucianism gained ground under Kubilai's successors**; even Mongol ruling-class women eventually lost freedom/power.

**Tolerance & foreign influence:**
- Court drew scholars/artists/artisans from many lands. **Persians and Turks** in the inner circle of advisors.
- **Muslims** designed/supervised the Chinese-style imperial city; proposed **more efficient tax collection**.
- **Persian astronomers** corrected the **Chinese calendar**, made highly accurate maps, set up **imperial hospitals**, and translated **36 volumes on Muslim medicine** for the imperial library.
- Religious tolerance: Buddhists, Nestorian Christians, Daoists, Latin Christians welcomed.
- **Marco Polo** (Venice): traveled ~1271–1295 with uncles; served as a **count/favorite of Kubilai's court**; his *Book of Marvels of the World* (may include borrowed fantastic tales) sparked European interest in Asia and later inspired **Columbus**.

**Social policies & scholar-gentry resistance:**
- Exams stayed canceled → Confucian scholars couldn't dominate government → **scholar-gentry alienated**.
- Mongols favored **artisans** (traditionally spared for their skills) and **merchants** (commerce boomed; **paper money** expanded; transportation improved) — both low-prestige in Confucian ideology.
- Built a **substantial navy** with "amazing speed" despite no seafaring tradition — used vs. pirates, Japan, Vietnam.
- Urban expansion continued (Tang/Song pattern); elites loved urban diversions.
- Traditional **poetry and calligraphy languished**, but **theater/drama flourished** (fictionalized romances & adventure stories) for court, merchants, Mongol elite; **actors and actresses** achieved social fame (long relegated by Confucians).
- **Pro-peasant policies:** forbade Mongols from converting cropland to pasture; restored/extended **irrigation projects** neglected in late Song; eased labor burdens by redirecting payments from **local tax farmers** to government officials; developed **rudimentary village education**.
- **First true guns used in China: 1290s** (late Yuan era).

## 4.2 The Fall of the House of Yuan

**Causes of collapse:**
- Mongols ruled all of China for **nine decades** (~1279–1368); weakness already visible by the **end of Kubilai's reign**.
- **Song loyalist revolts** in the south; defeats by **Japan** and **Vietnamese guerrillas** tarnished the aura of invincibility.
- Later Yuan leaders were **weak, uninterested in administration**; **Muslim and Chinese functionaries grew rich through graft and corruption**; **rising taxes + forced labor** angered the peasantry.
- **Scholar-gentry** called on the people to overthrow the "barbarian" usurpers.
- By the **1350s**: banditry and piracy rampant.

**The rebellions:**
- **White Lotus Society** — secret religious society dedicated to **overthrowing the Yuan**; leaders claimed **magical powers** to heal followers and confound enemies.
- Rebel leaders quarreled among themselves → chaos; surviving Mongols fled into **Central Asia**.

**The Ming restoration (1368):**
- Founder came from an "unexpected quarter": **Zhu Yuanzhang** = a **peasant** orphaned by famine/epidemics who had been a **young monastic novice (monk)** begging for food; left the monastery in the **late 1340s** to join a rebel band; rose through courage and leadership.
- **Ming dynasty: 1368–1644.** Zhu took the imperial title **Hongwu** (reign 1368–1398).
- Like the Han founder, a peasant emperor → **suspicious of the scholar-gentry** but knew he needed them:
  - Confucian scholars reappointed to the **highest offices**; state subsidies for imperial academies/colleges restored.
  - **Civil service exams RESTORED** — played a **greater role under Ming/Qing than under any earlier dynasty**.
- **Hongwu's anti-court-faction reforms:**
  - **Eliminated the office of chief minister** (key link between ministries) — powers transferred to the emperor.
  - **Public beatings/whippings** of officials paraded before assembled courtiers (many died of wounds).
  - **Removed aristocratic relatives** from power positions (to kill palace cliques).
  - Warned against **eunuchs** gaining independent power; limited their numbers in the **Forbidden City**.
  - **Thought control:** had sections of Confucian writings that displeased him **deleted from the imperial exam texts**.
- **Pro-peasant measures:** reduced taxes, redistributed land, promoted **silk and cotton handicrafts** as supplemental household income.

**Gender under the Ming (Visualizing the Past — Foot-binding):**
- **Foot-binding** spread from elite taste for small feet (possibly origins in a Tang emperor's delight in a dancing girl's tiny feet).
- Mothers bound daughters' feet starting **age 5–6**; toes turned under, bound with **silk**, wound tighter as the girl grew → "lotus petal" / "golden lily" feet.
- Result: **lifelong pain, crippled mobility** → confined women to the household; limited to home-based work like **textile production**.
- **Lower classes were slow to adopt** it (they needed women's labor in fields/markets) — but it became **vital to winning a good husband** for elite families.
- Passed **mother → daughter** across generations, nearly unresistable.
- Neo-Confucianism **more influential under Ming than Song**; yet elite women still influenced rulers — e.g., **Empress Ma** rebuked Hongwu for scolding her interest in the common people: *"You are the father of the people; I am the mother."*
- A woman's advancement hinged on **bearing male children** and rising from **daughter-in-law to mother-in-law** status.

## 4.3 The Rise of the Samurai in Japan

**Political structure 1200–1450:**
- Emperor kept (still called "Son of Heaven") but real power = **warlord military coalitions**; imperial bureaucracy designs never materialized; **no scholar-gentry elite** emerged; indigenous Japanese norms beat Chinese imports.
- Feudal-style power grew from **landed estates**; **bushi** = regional warrior leaders ruling **mini-states from fortresses** (wooden/earthen walls, moat-like ditches; rice storehouses, stables, wells, armories); they **administered law, supervised public works, collected revenue — for themselves**, not the court.
- **Samurai** = mounted troops **loyal to local lords, not the emperor**.
- 11th–12th c.: bandits roamed; Buddhist monasteries hired armed toughs; courts hired **samurai as bodyguards** → rise of warrior class.
- Weapons: **longbows and spears** until the 12th c., then the famous **curved steel swords**.
- Combat = **heroic duels between champions**; time and place **negotiated in advance**; battles chaotic with few fatalities.
- **Seppuku** (*hara-kiri*) = ritual suicide/disembowelment; showed courage, restored family honor.
- Rigid class barriers: **peasants banned from carrying swords or riding horses**; peasant poverty → appeal of the **Pure Land sect** (bliss in heaven for upright lives).

**The Gempei Wars and the Kamakura shogunate:**
- **Gempei Wars (1180–1185):** **Taira vs. Minamoto** families on Honshu island.
  - Taira had controlled the emperor and dominated court.
  - **Minamoto won by 1185** (superior provincial alliances); Taira destroyed.
- **Yoritomo** (Minamoto leader): so paranoid about overthrow he **exiled/killed his own relatives, including his brother Yoshitsune** (military genius of the war) → left **no able heir** → bushi lords scrambled for power.
- **Kamakura shogunate (1185–1333):** military government (**bakufu**) at **Kamakura on the Kanto plain**, east of the imperial court at **Kyoto**. Emperor preserved as figurehead.
- **Three-tiered fiction of power:** the **Hojo family** (warrior family allied to the Minamoto) **manipulated the Minamoto shoguns**, who claimed to rule in the name of the **emperor at Kyoto**.

**The Ashikaga shift:**
- **Ashikaga Takuaji** (Minamoto family member) **overthrew the Kamakura regime in 1336** → **Ashikaga shogunate (1336–1573)**.
- The emperor refused to recognize him → **driven from Kyoto to Yoshino**, where he and his heirs fought the Ashikaga **puppet emperors** at Kyoto for the rest of the 14th century (the "two courts" split).

**Thinking Historically — Comparing Feudalisms (Japan vs. Western Europe):**
- Shared: mutual ties & obligations, **militarism**, warrior virtues (courage, loyalty, ritualized combat, contempt for peasants/merchants), decentralized power with weak kings.
- **West = contractual** (explicit negotiated contracts) → legacy: **parliamentary institutions + lawyers**.
- **Japan = loyalty-based, not contractual** → legacy: **group consciousness / collective decision-making**.
- NOT truly feudal: Russia, Zhou China, Sudanic African kingdoms (decentralized, but no real feudal hierarchy).
- Possible shared later outcomes: both became industrial/capitalist success stories, imperialist, prone to war; links between persistent feudalism and **1930s right-wing militarism in Japan & Germany**.

## 4.4 Vietnam: Resistance and Expansionism

**Defeating the Mongols:**
- Mongols attacked Vietnam **BEFORE Japan** — **three major campaigns: 1258, 1285, and 1287–1288** — to force Vietnam to stop aiding Chinese resistance and become vassals.
- Vietnamese strategy: **guerrilla resistance** — avoided set battles; **ambushes and hit-and-run attacks**; let **hunger, tropical heat, incessant rainfall, and exhaustion** weaken the Mongols, then the **regular Vietnamese army struck** and routed them.
- Cost: towns destroyed, peasantry ravaged. Vietnam paid **tribute for a few years** and sent a **token member of the royal family to Beijing** — then **stopped behaving like subjects**.

**The Tran dynasty (1225–1400):**
- Came to power by **plotting to unseat a princess** who had inherited the previous dynasty.
- Independent for **300+ years** by the time of the Mongol invasions; resistance capacity owed much to **nearly 10 centuries of Chinese rule** (administrative/organizational borrowing).
- Post-invasion devastation pushed **FURTHER sinification**: a state and society modeled on China, incl. a **Confucian-style examination system**.
- Limits: Tran emperors lacked resources for a **village-level bureaucracy** — **village notables** stayed dominant (but unlike Japan, they did NOT build armed bands → emperors kept control of the military).
- Court supported **Buddhist monks/monasteries**; Buddhist teachings pervaded popular culture (see *The Tale of Kieu*, Vietnam's beloved literary classic).

**Vietnamese women:**
- Held a **stronger position than Chinese women** (more family and social influence) — Vietnamese society favored nuclear households (vs. Chinese extended family), like most of SE Asia.
- **Ho Xuan Huong** — famous woman poet who **ridiculed polygamy** in "Sharing a Husband" ("One wife is covered by a quilted blanket, while one wife is left in the cold...").

**Expansionism southward:**
- Vietnamese **avoided the malarial highlands**; expanded **south along the coast**, using their Chinese-derived **population and bureaucratic/military organization**.
- **Chams** — Indianized rivals (Hindu-Buddhist high culture, centralized state) but **lacked Chinese-derived technologies incl. firearms** → defeated, driven into the **highlands** (descendants live there today).
- **Khmers** — Indianized rivals centered in the interior; moving into the **Mekong delta** as Vietnamese advanced; Vietnamese occupied much of the **upper Mekong delta** by the time the **French arrived in force in the late 18th century** (pushing into modern Cambodia).

## Global Connections: The East Asian Corner of the Global System

- **Chinese model** spread writing, bureaucracy, religion, art to **Japan, Korea, Vietnam** — all fertile wet-rice lowlands.
- Chinese imports were **monopolized by elites** — except **popular Buddhism**, which reached the masses; Buddhism (Indian origin, filtered through China) was the key link between these civilizations.
- Different outcomes: **Korea** = brief direct rule → symbolic submission + cultural dependence; **Vietnam** = 1000+ yrs of Chinese rule → independence struggle + Chinese culture as counterweight to Indianized rivals; **Japan** = never conquered → warlords limited Chinese influence, indigenous ways reasserted.
- China remained the **epitome of civilization** for the region → sense that no other civilization was worth emulating → **tendency toward isolation** from the wider world (even during Mongol rule).
