## 12.1 Spaniards and Portuguese: From Reconquest to Conquest (p. 302)

- **Iberian background:** the peninsula had long been a frontier between Christianity and Islam, producing a strong tradition of **military conquest and rule over peoples of other faiths**.
- **Christian kingdoms:** Portugal on the Atlantic, Aragon in the east, and **Castile** — the largest — in the center.
- **Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile** carried out a program of unification; the fall of **Granada (1492)**, the last Muslim kingdom, completed the reconquest.
- **Religious unification:** Isabella ordered Jews to convert or leave; as many as **200,000** may have departed, disrupting parts of the Castilian economy.
- **1492:** with Granada taken and religious unity enforced, the monarchs backed **Columbus's** plan to reach the East Indies by sailing west.
- **Result by the 1570s:** much of the Americas was under Iberian control; American realities and native resistance modified Iberian traditions but did not erase them.

### 12.1.1 Iberian Society and Tradition

- **Urban society:** like Mediterranean peoples, Spaniards and Portuguese were heavily urban; Europeans in America lived in towns surrounded by a rural native population.
- **Social ambition:** commoners who came as conquerors hoped to remake themselves as a **new nobility**, with native peoples as their serfs.
- **Patriarchal family** transferred easily to Latin America, where large estates and grants of Indian labor supported relations based on economic dominance.
- **Slavery:** Iberia had kept slaves as part of its frontier experience (unlike most of medieval Europe), and African slaves came through the trans-Saharan trade; slavery extended to America on that foundation.
- **Government:** centralization depended on a professional bureaucracy of trained lawyers and judges (comparable to China and other great empires).
- **Church and state:** religion was the other pillar of Iberian politics; close links (including royal nomination of church officials) were extended to the New World.
- **Merchant tradition:** Portugal had moved down the African coast since 1415, building **trading posts** rather than colonies; in the **Atlantic islands** it established estates, a slave trade, and commercial **sugar** agriculture. Brazil would repeat that shift from trade factory to plantation.

### 12.1.2 The Chronology of Conquest

- **Three periods:**
  1. **1492–c. 1570** — conquest; main lines of administration and economy set.
  2. **1570–c. 1700** — consolidation and maturity; colonial institutions and societies take form.
  3. **18th century** — reform and reorganization, which intensified colonial rule and planted **seeds of dissatisfaction and revolt**.
- The first period brought vast areas and millions of people under European control within about a century; immigration, commerce, and exploitation tied them to an emerging **Atlantic economy**.
- **Mexico and Peru** attracted Spaniards because of their large sedentary populations and mineral resources; other conquests radiated outward from those centers.

### 12.1.3 The Caribbean Crucible

- **1493:** a return expedition established a colony on **Santo Domingo (Hispaniola)**.
- **Encomienda:** the agricultural **Taino** produced enough surplus labor for the Spanish to distribute people to individual Spaniards in a kind of serfdom. An **encomendero** could use the people as workers or tax them.
- **Collapse:** gold hunting, slaving, and European disease depopulated the islands; within two decades little was left to hold Spanish attention.
- Spain occupied **Cuba, Hispaniola, and Puerto Rico** (not the lesser Antilles); fortified ports such as **Havana, San Juan, and Santo Domingo** guarded the commercial lifeline. The Caribbean then became a backwater until **sugar and slaves** revived it; in the 17th century the English, French, and Dutch settled the smaller islands.
- **Testing ground:** in the 40 years between Columbus and the conquest of Mexico, the **Columbian Exchange** began in earnest, and Spanish institutions were adapted to American conditions.
- **Cities:** laid out on a **grid plan** with a central plaza holding the town hall, main church, and governor's palace (Roman models, rational planning); conquest came to imply **settlement**.
- **Institutions:** governorship, treasury office, and a royal court of appeals staffed by magistrates; notaries and a body of law based on Castile; the church (Dominicans) took part; a cathedral was rising on Hispaniola by **1530**, soon followed by a university.
- **Society:** immigration came from Castile and other Iberian kingdoms; by the 1510s more **Spanish women** arrived; Spanish and Italian merchants imported **African slaves** for island sugar plantations.
- **Shift:** when the gold phase ended in the **1520s**, ranches and sugar plantations replaced it; the Taino were nearly gone within about 30 years, while the **Caribs** of the Lesser Antilles held out longer.
- **Reformers:** as early as **1510**, clerics and royal officials tried to end the worst abuses; **Bartolomé de Las Casas**, a conquistador turned Dominican priest, began his campaign for justice.

### 12.1.4 The Paths of Conquest

- **Scale:** expeditions usually numbered **50 to 500 men**; women, missionaries, administrators, and artisans followed to form civil society. Conquest was a series of individual initiatives with government approval.
- **Two prongs:** one toward **Mexico**, one toward **South America**.
- **Mexico:** **Hernán Cortés** led **600 men** inland in **1519**; after defeating towns subject to the Aztecs he won their support. With Indian allies he reached **Tenochtitlan**, captured and killed **Moctezuma II**, was driven out, then besieged the city; disease, starvation, and battle brought it down in **1521**. **Mexico City** was built on its ruins.
- By **1535** most of Central Mexico was under Spanish control, becoming the core of the **viceroyalty of New Spain** (Central America, the Caribbean, the Philippines, and the area from the present Southwest United States to Panama).
- **Peru:** **Francisco Pizarro** conquered the **Inca** Empire in **1532**, helped by a recent civil war; fewer than **200 Spaniards** plus native allies brought it down. **Cuzco** fell in **1533**, but the Spanish built **Lima** nearer the coast; most of Peru was controlled by **1540**, with resistance in remote areas for another 30 years.
- **Frontiers:** **Coronado (1540–1542)** searched for golden cities as far as Kansas; **Pedro de Valdivia** conquered the Araucanians of Chile and founded **Santiago (1541)**; **Buenos Aires** (1536) was abandoned and refounded in **1580**; Amazon expeditions found little to settle.
- By **1570** there were **192 Spanish towns** in the Americas, a third in Mexico and Central America.

### 12.1.5 The Conquerors

- **Power:** captains ruled by force of will — "God in heaven, the king in Spain, and me here." The crown took **one-fifth** of all treasure.
- **Rewards:** men signed on for **shares**; horses or special skills earned double shares; leaders favored friends, relatives, and men from their home province, leaving dissatisfied conquerors ready to launch new expeditions.
- **Backgrounds:** few were professional soldiers; they came from all ranks of Spanish life, occasionally including former slaves and freedmen, and rarely a few women such as **Inés Suarez** in Chile.
- **Motives:** men on the make, seeking wealth and the conversion of the heathen; most were satisfied with **encomiendas** and saw themselves as a new nobility over a new peasantry.
- **Why Spain succeeded:** **horses, firearms, and steel** against stone technology; ruthless leadership; **epidemic disease**; and **internal divisions** within highly centralized native empires, which gave Spain native allies. Mobile **nomadic** peoples resisted longest.
- **End of the phase:** by about **1570** bureaucrats, merchants, and colonists replaced the conquerors; Peru had a civil war in the **1540s**, but viceroys and law courts signaled that Spanish America was now a **colony**, not a conquest.

### 12.1.6 Conquest and Morality

- **Questions raised:** Who were the Indians? Were they fully human? Should they be converted? Could conquest be justified?
- **Sépulveda (1548):** drawing on Aristotle, argued the conquest was just — Spain freed the Indians from unjust lords, brought salvation, and the Indians were "born to serve."
- **Valladolid (1550):** the king suspended further conquests and convened a commission. **Las Casas** answered that Indians were rational people who had never harmed Christians, that conquest of their lands was unjust, and that conversion must be **peaceful**.
- **Outcome:** mixed — Sépulveda's book was censored, but conquests continued; some abuses were moderated, yet it was "too little, too late." Spain's concern with legality, and voices like Las Casas, are still part of the story.

### Document — A Vision from the Vanquished (pp. 308–309)

- **Guaman Poma de Ayala**, a Christian, Quechua- and Spanish-speaking Peruvian of Inca noble descent, wrote a memorial in the **17th century** for **Philip III**, hoping the king would end abuses. It was unpublished until the 20th century and included drawings.
- **Charges:** the **mita** and mine owners torture and kill Indian nobles; workers get **quicksilver poisoning** and die; **encomenderos** live in luxury off Indian labor and evade audits; Indian nobles are treated as commoners though they are loyal Christian vassals.
- **Significance:** a rare detailed critique of the empire **from an Indian point of view**, not of the laws but of how they worked in practice.

## 12.2 The Destruction and Transformation of Indigenous Societies (p. 310)

- **Demographic catastrophe:** the Caribbean's native population nearly disappeared by **1540**; **Peru fell from 10 million to 1.5 million** (1530–1590); **Central Mexico fell from about 25 million (1519) to under 2 million (1580)**.
- **Causes:** smallpox, influenza, and measles, to which native peoples had no immunity; conquest and the weakening of societies added to the loss.
- **Consequences:** in Central Mexico survivors were concentrated into fewer towns, and former communal lands were seized by Spanish landowners; maintaining traditional social and economic structures became very difficult.
- **Livestock:** cattle, sheep, and horses multiplied on new Spanish farms — in a shocking way, European animals replaced an indigenous population on the land.

### 12.2.1 Exploitation of the Indians

- **Selective destruction:** the old religions and priesthood were eliminated, but the **indigenous nobility** was kept as middlemen between Spanish tax and labor demands and the majority.
- **Slavery:** enslavement of Indians (except those taken in war) was banned in most of Spanish America by the mid-16th century; other forms of labor and tribute replaced it.
- **Encomienda:** at first grants to individual conquerors, who could use or tax their dependents; demands were arbitrary and often excessive, and the system was destructive. The crown, fearing a new nobility, **ended the encomienda in the 1540s** (limiting inheritance and labor demands); it was all but gone by the **1620s** in central Mexico and Peru.
- **Land over people:** colonists increasingly wanted **land grants** rather than Indians as the basis of wealth.
- **Mita:** communities had to send laborers for state projects — churches, roads, mining, agriculture. In Peru the **mita**, adapted from Inca precedent, mobilized thousands for the mines; workers were paid but abuses were common.
- **Wage labor:** by the 17th century many Indians left villages to escape obligations, working for landowners or in cities → growth of a **wage labor system**.
- **Resilience:** native peoples learned to use Spanish courts, and local life retained many indigenous features; they adopted European foods and technology selectively.

## 12.3 Colonial Economies and Governments (p. 311)

- **Agrarian society:** perhaps **80%** of the population lived and worked on the land, yet **mining** was the essential activity for Spain and the basis of its rule.
- **Precious metals** first tied Latin America into the developing **world economy**; gold came from the Caribbean, Colombia, and Chile, but **silver** was the foundation of Spanish wealth.

### 12.3.1 The Silver Heart of Empire

- **Discoveries:** major silver finds in Mexico and Peru between **1545 and 1565**; **Potosi** (upper Peru, modern Bolivia) was the largest, producing about **80% of Peruvian silver**; over **160,000** people lived in the town and mine in the early 17th century. **Zacatecas** was Mexico's great center.
- **Cost:** a viceroy of Peru said Spain received not silver but "the blood and sweat of Indians."
- **Labor:** early native slaves and encomienda workers gave way to **labor drafts**; by **1572** the mita supplied about **13,000 workers a year** to Potosi. By the 17th century there were also many **wage workers**.
- **Technology:** European methods; after **1580** silver was extracted by **amalgamation with mercury**. The mercury mountain at **Huancavelica** made Potosi and Huancavelica the "great marriage of Peru."
- **Law and revenue:** subsoil rights belonged to the crown; individuals mined and paid **one-fifth** of production, while the crown also monopolized mercury.
- **Spin-offs:** mines had to be fed and supplied — Mexican cattle, sheep, and wheat farms; for Peru, mercury, mules, food, clothing, and **coca** leaves came from distant regions.

### 12.3.2 Haciendas and Villages

- **Land:** as native populations fell, Spanish ranches and farms spread, and **land ownership** became more attractive than labor grants.
- **Haciendas:** family-owned rural estates producing grains, grapes, and livestock, worked largely by Indians who had left their communities and by people of mixed heritage.
- **Role:** haciendas supplied consumers in America and became the **basis of wealth and power** for the local aristocracy; exported plantation crops (sugar, later cacao) were small next to silver.
- **Competition:** where native communities kept their lands, an enduring contest between **haciendas and villages** developed.

### 12.3.3 Industry and Commerce

- **Textiles:** sheep raising led to small **textile sweatshops**, usually staffed by women. America was self-sufficient in basic foods and goods and imported mainly luxuries.
- **Monopoly:** only Spaniards could trade with America; trade passed through **Seville** (after 1710, **Cadiz**), supervised by a Board of Trade and the merchant guild (**consulado**) of Seville, which kept colonial prices high.
- **Convoys:** two fleets sailed annually, traded goods for precious metals, met at **Havana**, and returned under guard of **galleons**; two Manila galleons a year brought Chinese silks, porcelain, and lacquer to Mexico. Fortified ports (Havana, Cartagena) sheltered the ships. The system ran until the **1730s** and lost only one fleet (to the Dutch, **1627**).
- **Contraband** with foreigners flourished despite the rules.
- **Reality of the treasure:** much of the silver left Spain for wars, debts, and manufactures; probably **less than half stayed in Spain**. It caused inflation (the price revolution) in Spain and then Western Europe, and never supplied more than **one-fourth** of Spain's state revenues. As **Sancho de Moncada** wrote in 1619, "the poverty of Spain resulted from the discovery of the Indies."

### Thinking Historically — An Atlantic History (pp. 314–315)

- **Definition:** the continuous contact after **1492** turned the Atlantic from a barrier into a **highway** linking Europe, Africa, and the Americas.
- **Scale:** between **1492 and 1850**, **10–15 million Africans** and over **5 million Europeans** crossed to the Americas.
- **Exchanges:** peoples carried traditions and adapted them; crops, animals, and **pathogens** crossed (with disastrous epidemics in the Americas); **Alfred Crosby** described this Columbian Exchange as transforming life on four continents.
- **Model:** Fernand **Braudel's** Mediterranean history (1959) inspired similar work on the Indian Ocean; **Atlantic history** emerged in the 1970s to study connections rather than isolated continents.
- **Differences:** the Atlantic is far larger and more varied than the Mediterranean and lacked the Indian Ocean's older indigenous linkages — it grew out of European empires and trade.
- **Culture and ideas:** a "Christian Atlantic" of Catholic and Protestant missions; African religions survived as **voudon** (Haiti), **candomblé** (Brazil), **obeah** (Jamaica); circulation of Enlightenment ideas helped produce revolutions in the United States, France, Haiti, and Latin America.
- **Debate:** some see Atlantic unity ending with independence; others extend it to the end of the slave trades (c. 1860) or abolition (1888). Critics say the frame is **too narrow**, since Mexican silver reached China and Asian spices reached Europe.

### 12.3.4 Ruling an Empire: State and Church

- **Sovereignty:** rested with the crown on the basis of a **papal grant** to Castile; the **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)** drew a north–south line, giving lands east of it (including **Brazil**) to Portugal and lands west to Spain.
- **Bureaucracy:** staffed largely by **letrados**, university-trained lawyers; judicial officers also exercised legislative and administrative power. Laws were codified in the **Recopilación (1681)**.
- **Structure:** the king ruled through the **Council of the Indies** in Spain; in America, two **viceroyalties** (Mexico City and Lima) under **viceroys**; ten **audiencias** (high courts) staffed by professional magistrates; local magistrates collected taxes and assigned Indian labor.
- **Church:** religious orders — **Franciscans, Dominicans, Jesuits** — converted Indians and founded missions; some missionaries defended Indian rights. **Sahagún** mastered Nahuatl and compiled a bilingual encyclopedia of Aztec culture; **Diego de Landa** burned Maya books and tortured suspected backsliders.
- **Culture:** parishes and bishoprics replaced the missionary church in core areas; baroque cathedrals, printing presses, schools (Mexico City and Lima, 1550s), and eventually more than **70 universities**. **Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz (1651–1695)** — poet, author, musician — exemplified colonial intellectual life.
- **Inquisition:** offices in major capitals enforced orthodoxy; Indians were exempt, but converted Jews, Protestants, and other dissenters were prosecuted.
- **Fusion:** native beliefs blended with Christianity (e.g., worship of Mary alongside an Aztec fertility goddess); a new Latin American culture emerged from mixed ingredients, not simply from Europe.

## 12.4 Brazil: The First Plantation Colony (p. 317)

- **1500:** **Pedro Alvares Cabral**, leading an expedition to India, made a landfall on Brazil (perhaps accidental). Only **dyewood** attracted interest, so the crown neglected Brazil for 30 years, licensing merchants instead.
- **French competition** pushed the crown to act: the coast was cleared of rivals and settlement organized in **1532** through **captaincies** — strips of land granted to minor nobles with broad powers but little capital; only a few succeeded with towns, colonists, peaceful relations, and **sugar plantations** using native, then African, slaves.
- **1549:** a governor general founded the royal capital at **Salvador**; Jesuits arrived. By **1600** indigenous resistance was broken in many places, settlements lined the coast, and about **150 plantations** (doubling by 1630) worked increasingly with African slaves.
- **Population (1600):** about **100,000** — 30,000 Europeans, 15,000 black slaves, the rest natives and people of mixed origin.

### 12.4.1 Sugar and Slavery

- **Leading producer:** Brazil dominated world sugar for most of the 17th century. Cane had to be cut and pressed in mills and the juice heated — agriculture plus industry in the field, requiring capital and heavy labor.
- **Slaves:** about **7,000 a year** were imported in the 17th century; by 1700 Brazil had roughly **150,000 slaves**, about **half its population**.
- **Model:** a single slave-grown crop made Brazil the first great **plantation colony**, copied later by other Europeans in the Caribbean; its social hierarchy long reflected plantation origins — white planter families as an aristocracy, slaves at the bottom, and a growing mixed population of artisans, small farmers, herders, and free laborers.
- **Government:** two colonies — **Maranhão** (Amazon) and **Brazil**; a governor general at Salvador, though captaincy governors often acted independently; the **Jesuits** ran missions, cattle ranches, and sugar mills.
- **Dependency:** unlike Spanish America, Brazil had **no universities or printing presses**, so intellectual life depended on Portugal; its economic dependency was matched by intellectual subordination.

### 12.4.2 Brazil's Age of Gold

- **1580–1640:** the Habsburg kings of Spain also ruled Portugal. The **Dutch** seized northeastern Brazil (**1630–1654**) and its sugar; though expelled in 1654, Dutch, English, and French Caribbean plantations then undercut Brazilian sugar — slave prices rose and sugar prices fell.
- **Paulistas:** backwoodsmen from **São Paulo** explored the interior, captured Indians, and searched for metals, establishing Portuguese claims.
- **1695:** gold strikes at **Minas Gerais** set off a gold rush; coastal towns and plantations were deserted, and about **5,000 immigrants a year** arrived from Portugal. Slaves worked the mines; by **1775** Minas Gerais had over **150,000 slaves** in a population of 300,000.
- **Towns and control:** mining camps became towns such as **Ouro Préto**; the crown taxed heavily; gold output peaked **1735–1760** at about **3 tons a year**, making Brazil the greatest gold source in the Western world; diamonds followed.
- **Effects:** the interior opened to settlement (again disastrous for indigenous peoples), slavery expanded, coastal agriculture was disrupted, and ranching and farming grew to supply the mines. **Rio de Janeiro** became the colony's capital in **1763**.
- **Long run:** gold let Portugal buy manufactured goods instead of industrializing; much of it passed to **England**; after **1760**, as gold dwindled, Portugal slid into economic dependency on England.

## 12.5 Multiracial Societies (p. 320)

- **Three peoples, unequal conditions:** Europeans came as conquerors and voluntary immigrants, Indians as conquered peoples, Africans as slaves → hierarchies of masters and servants, Christians and pagans.
- **Two "republics":** in theory, a "republic of the Spaniards" (all non-Indians) and a "republic of the Indians" with its own ranks and laws. In practice the separation never existed, and the Indian republic was the base on which society rested; Indians paid tribute, unlike others (except mulattos in some places).
- **Castas:** mixture created multiracial societies ranked by color, status, and occupation; by the 18th century the **castas** were a major segment of the population.

### 12.5.1 The Society of Castas

- **Miscegenation:** conquest brought sexual exploitation of Indian women, occasional alliances, and some marriages (especially with the indigenous nobility); with few European women, mixed unions were common.
- **Mestizos:** a large mixed population emerged, suspected of illegitimacy but ranked above Indians; acculturated and able to move between worlds, they became an intermediate group expected to live by Spanish standards and often serving Spanish society.
- **Brazil and the Caribbean:** slave owners exploited female slaves and sometimes freed their mulatto children, producing another large mixed population.
- **Sociedad de castas:** a hierarchy based on racial origin — whites at the top, black slaves and Native Americans at the bottom, many mixed categories between. Terms varied locally; a genre of **casta painting** catalogued the combinations.
- **Growth:** castas were perhaps **5–10%** of Spanish America's population in **1650**, but **35–40%** by **1750**. In Brazil, free people of color were about **28%**, equal to whites, while free and slave blacks and mulattos together were **two-thirds** of the population.
- **Mobility:** restrictions increased, but a successful Indian might call himself a mestizo, and a mestizo marrying a Spaniard might be called white; freed slaves and detribalized Indians swelled the castas.
- **Color mattered:** whites held most wealth and office — "In America, every white is a gentleman."
- **Peninsulares and Creoles:** **peninsulares** were born in Spain; **Creoles** were whites born in the New World. Creoles dominated local economies and ranked just below peninsulares, but their status was suspect; growing pride and resentment of discrimination later fed **independence movements**.
- **Gender and family:** fathers had legal authority over children until age 25; women were subordinate, barred from government, and expected to run the household. Wives came under husbands' authority, though widows often took charge; lower-class women traded, farmed, and worked in textile workshops. Marriages were often arranged with a **dowry** that stayed the woman's property, and women had inheritance rights; some upper-class women were placed in convents.

### Visualizing the Past — Race or Culture? A Changing Society (p. 321)

- Graphs for Peru and Mexico show the **casta share of the population rising** while the Indian share fell through the 18th century.
- **Caution:** the categories were not strictly biological — an Indian who spoke Spanish or dressed in Spanish style might be counted as a casta, so precise-looking numbers rest on **imprecise social definition**.
- **Casta paintings** show husband, wife, and child of different categories; they reveal domestic life and material culture as much as racial ideology.
