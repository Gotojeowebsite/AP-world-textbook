## Intro — Silver and the New World Economy (p. 252)

- **Silver:** became the global currency of the Early Modern period; its production and use show the power of the world economy emerging after 1500.
- **Potosi (Bolivia):** mid-16th-century find, an enormous vein of ore; by **1600** about **150,000 miners** worked there, over a third conscripted through the **mita** — the Inca labor draft revived by the Spanish.
- **Spanish crown:** kept **one-fifth** of colonial silver = its chief gain from the American colonies; paid for armies and grand public buildings.
- **Where the silver went:** most passed through Spain to merchants elsewhere → spent on Asian goods (Indian spices, Chinese porcelain and silk); some went to the Philippines and was traded for Chinese products.
- **China and India were the largest recipients** → a sign of Asia's dynamism in the new economy. Silver replaced paper money in China; merchants demanded it; Ming taxes were paid in silver, reducing the number of collections — a reform called **"one whip of the lash."** Silver helped keep Chinese living standards above Western Europe's into the early 19th century.
- **Criticism:** Chinese observers worried silver widened the gap between rich and poor; the poor struggled to obtain the silver their taxes required. Few Europeans worried about the harsh conditions in the mines.
- **Chapter argument:** European voyages and conquests shifted world power, but they also **redefined exchanges among major societies**; Europe's role was disproportionate, yet African and Asian contributions were active too.

## 10.1 The West's First Outreach: Maritime Power (p. 253)

- **Growing awareness (since 1100):** the Crusades revealed the wealth of the Islamic world and the goods available from Asia; the Mongol Empire sped up exchanges; the fall of the khans made China a mystery to Europeans again.
- **Existing route:** Asian spices and goods traveled in Arab ships to the Middle East, overland, then in Genoese and Venetian fleets across the Mediterranean.
- **European disadvantages:** ignorance of the wider world; the **Vikings** had reached Greenland and Vinland around 1000 but lost interest (indigenous resistance, little gain); fear of sailing off the world's edge; fear of the rising **Ottoman Empire**; **lack of gold** to pay for Asian imports; Mediterranean oar-driven ships could not cross open oceans.

### 10.1.1 New Technology: A Key to Power

- **Ships:** deep-draft, round-hulled sailing ships built for the Atlantic that could carry heavy armaments.
- **Navigation:** improved compass, maps, and instruments; new combinations of sails (the **caravel**) let ships use different winds → mid-ocean travel, greater speed, flexibility even in the Indian Ocean.
- **Weapons:** Chinese gunpowder plus advancing European metalwork → the first guns and cannons. Inaccurate, but awesome by the standards of the time.
- **Result:** the West gained a **military advantage at sea** over all other civilizations, which it kept into the 20th century.

### 10.1.2 Portugal and Spain Lead the Pack

- **Portugal:** Atlantic location suited it; motives mixed curiosity, desire to harm the Muslim world, and thirst for wealth. **Prince Henry the Navigator (1394–1460)** organized annual expeditions down the African coast and out to the Azores.
- From **1434** the Portuguese pushed farther down the African coast each voyage, bringing back slaves, pepper, and stories of gold.
- **1488:** rounded the **Cape of Good Hope**; weary sailors forced the expedition back before India.
- **1492:** Columbus's voyage for Spain made Portugal redouble its efforts.
- **1498 — Vasco da Gama** reached India with four ships and a Hindu pilot picked up in East Africa. The Portuguese mistook Hindu temples for churches; Muslim merchants who dominated the trade were hostile; the Portuguese had only crude goods (iron pots) and some gold, but returned with a small spice cargo.
- **Force replaced trade goods:** later voyages used ships' guns; da Gama killed or tortured Indian merchants to set an example.
- **After da Gama:** annual Portuguese voyages; Brazil claimed after a fleet was blown off course; forts on the African coast and in India (**Mozambique, Goa**); **1514** reached Indonesia (the spice center) and China; **1542** reached Japan and began a mission.
- **Spain:** recently united and just reconquered from Muslim rule → missionary zeal plus desire for riches. **Christopher Columbus**, a Genoese captain sailing for the Spanish monarchy, went west in **1492**, reached the Americas, and called the inhabitants "Indians." **Amerigo Vespucci** gave the New World its name.
- **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494):** a papal line divided the globe; **Brazil** fell to Portugal, most of the Americas to Spain.
- **Magellan (1519–1521):** first circumnavigation of the globe; on that basis Spain claimed the **Philippines**, which it held until 1898.
- **Empires:** Portugal held African coasts, Goa, Macao, brief Japan trade, and Brazil. Spain held the Philippines, Pacific islands, most of the Americas, Florida, and sent expeditions into California and the Southwest.

### 10.1.3 Northern European Expeditions

- Late 16th century: the lead passed to **France, England, and Holland**; Protestant states rivaled Catholic gains; Spain and Portugal were busy digesting their empires; the Dutch and British built lighter, faster ships.
- **1588:** Britain defeated the **Spanish Armada**; from then on Britain, Holland, and France contested dominance at sea.
- In the Americas they expanded mainly **northward**, since they could not challenge Iberian colonies — except in the sugar-rich **West Indies**, where they seized Spanish-claimed islands.
- **Profit became the dominant motive:** English explorers were told to watch for native peoples who could buy woolens, or for fishing grounds.
- **France:** reached Canada in **1534**; in the 17th century pushed into the Great Lakes and Mississippi valley.
- **England:** a brief 1497 expedition; searched for a northwest passage (Hudson Bay); serious colonization of the North American east coast began in the 17th century.
- **Holland:** entered after winning independence from Spain; ousted Portugal from Indonesia by the early 17th century; explored Australia; settled the southern tip of Africa as a **relay station** for East Indies voyages.
- **Joint-stock companies** (Dutch and British East India Companies): government-granted monopolies, barely supervised, with rights to raise armies and coin money — they acted almost like independent governments (the Dutch ruled Taiwan; the British East India Company ruled parts of India). They had larger, experienced bureaucracies than earlier Arab trading enterprises, relied less on family ties, and arranged production as well as commerce.
- **Hardships:** voyages lasted months or years; scurvy was rampant; crews sometimes despaired of surviving.

### Thinking Historically — Causation and the West's Expansion (p. 259)

- **Historical causation:** unlike scientific experiments, the past cannot be repeated, so no exact percentage can be assigned to a cause; historians disagree, but high probability is possible and sloppy claims can be disproved.
- **Single-cause "determinisms":** cultural (some anthropologists), technological, economic (including Marxist), and "**great men**" (Columbus, da Gama, Henry the Navigator).
- **Applied to expansion:**
  - *Great men:* daring and vision of explorers and their sponsors.
  - *Culture:* Renaissance innovation and a Christian missionary spirit.
  - *Politics:* rivalry among nation-states kept driving the search for routes and colonies.
  - *Technology:* naval cannons, navigation, and ship design → Europe advanced only where ships' guns could reach: **port cities, islands, and trade routes**, not inland.
- **Problem:** technological determinism begs "what caused the cause?" — why were Europeans so ready to adopt inventions, and why did China, aware of them, choose not to imitate? **Technology and culture worked together.**
- **European disadvantage** also mattered: few desirable exports and dislike of dependence on Muslim traders for Asian goods.
- **Conclusion:** no agreement on a precise ranking of causes, but the debate has moved beyond "great men" toward underlying contexts.

## 10.2 The Columbian Exchange of Disease and Food (p. 258)

- **Proto-globalization:** Europe's new routes added to existing transregional trade; including the Americas made exchange truly global.
- **Disease:** smallpox, measles, and influenza hit peoples with no immunities; **more than half** of Native Americans died, with some estimates as high as **80%**; whole West Indian island populations were wiped out. The devastation ran about **150 years**; the same pattern later struck Polynesians and Pacific Coast peoples in the 18th century.
- **American crops spread:** corn and sweet potatoes were widely adopted in **China** (long-term population growth from the 17th century); the **potato** reached Europe around 1700 and triggered population upheaval; about **30% of foods eaten today** come from American plants; corn became a staple in Africa.
- **European caution:** rumors spread that American foods caused disease; the potato took more than a century to gain ground, though fried potatoes were sold in Paris by the 1680s.
- **Animals and other crops:** horses, cattle, and sheep went to the Americas; Old World grains, African yams, and fruit trees such as apples went the other way.

### 10.2.1 Environment and Labor

- **Environment:** Europeans ignored local environmental knowledge and expanded output. **Sheep** close grazing devastated vegetation and caused erosion; **deforestation** increased — **25% of the world's forests** were cut by 1700 and **50% by 1850**; Latin American clearances rose **fivefold 1650–1750**.
- **Animals and seas:** the fur trade cut animal numbers (**beavers declined over 47%** in the 18th century alone); Russian fur traders pushed into Alaska for new sources; deep-sea whaling and heavy North Atlantic fishing (partly for cheap food for slaves) depleted species such as cod.
- **Labor:** demand for sugar plus a shortage of native workers from disease → experiments with **indentured servitude** failed to recruit enough people; the **African slave trade** became the major response, with millions shipped across the Atlantic.
- **Atlantic slavery was not merely an imitation of older systems:** far greater numbers were involved, and planters deliberately increased discipline and production.

## 10.3 Toward a World Economy (p. 261)

- Europeans did **not** displace all Asian shipping from Chinese and Japanese waters or monopolize the Indian Ocean; Muslim traders stayed active on the East African coast. But Western Europe controlled a growing share of **oceanic shipping**, using warships to muscle into trade between other societies (e.g., India and Southeast Asia) → higher profits and power to set the rules of trade.
- **Lepanto (1571):** a Spanish-led fleet defeated the Ottoman navy; the Ottomans rebuilt but could not challenge Europe on the main international routes.
- **Indian Ocean:** Europeans controlled about **50% of long-distance trade** by the 17th century.
- **Ports and colonies:** the Americas above all, plus a few in Africa and Asia; **Macao** in China; Western enclaves in **Constantinople**, **Moscow/St. Petersburg**, and Dutch access to **Nagasaki** in isolationist Japan.

### 10.3.1 Imbalances in World Trade

- **Spain** briefly dominated thanks to American silver but lacked a strong banking system; **England, France, and Holland**, with stronger merchant classes, captured most of the profits.
- **Core pattern:** Western Europe exported expensive finished goods (guns, cloth) in exchange for unprocessed goods (silver, sugar) — an extra margin of profit.
- **Mercantilism:** a nation should not import from outside its own empire, should export as widely as possible in its own ships, and should use tariffs to discourage colonial manufacturing.
- **Dependent regions** supplied precious metals and cash crops (sugar, spice, tobacco, later cotton); **sub-Saharan Africa** mainly supplied slaves, and the Atlantic replaced the Sahara as the main axis of West African trade.

### 10.3.2 A System of International Inequality

- **Durable system:** many regions made dependent by the 17th century still carry burdens in world trade today.
- **Not everyone was poor:** African slave traders and princes grew rich; Latin American merchants and farmers supplied the mines; many peasants remained outside the market economy altogether.
- **But dependent merchants and landlords did not control their own terms of trade;** their wealth went into imported European goods and luxuries rather than local manufacturing.
- **Coercive labor spread:** African slaves and indentured servants in the Americas; estate systems using natives and **mestizos**; forced labor for spices in the Dutch East Indies, at the Dutch Cape, and in British India by the 18th century.

### 10.3.3 How Much World in the World Economy?

- **China:** deliberately avoided involvement on someone else's terms; copied some firearms at a low level; used regulation and a coastal navy to keep Europeans in check; channeled most limited trade through **Macao**. Its economy stayed dynamic — porcelain was made to order for European designs — so Europeans paid with American silver. At the end of the 18th century a British mission seeking wider trade was rebuffed: China "had no need for outside goods."
- **Japan:** initially attracted by Western ships and guns (useful in feudal wars), then pulled back; leaders feared Western influence and the threat guns posed to **samurai** dominance. After building its own gun industry, Japan **cut most contact**, suppressed Christians, and from the 17th to the 19th century kept almost complete isolation except for Chinese contact and a Dutch enclave at **Nagasaki**. **Korea** also withdrew.
- **India:** Mughal rulers welcomed Western traders and allowed small port enclaves; India sold spices and cotton textiles. But the Mughals focused on internal, land-based expansion — world trade was a sideline. The **Ottoman** and **Safavid** empires were similar; **Russia** stayed partly outside the world economy until the 18th century, trading largely with Central Asian nomads.

### 10.3.4 The Expansionist Trend

- The world economy kept **gaining ground**: some West African regions became colonial dependencies from the 16th century; Southeast Asian export regions fell under Western trading companies by the 17th century.
- **India:** as the Mughal Empire weakened, the British and French East India Companies took growing roles in internal trade and administration. Britain taxed Indian cotton cloth to protect its own industry; by the late 18th century India was a market for British goods and a source of gold payments. Indian manufacturing began to decline, though India kept a complex regional economy and was never as dependent as Latin America.
- **Eastern Europe:** growing Western cities created demand for imported grain by the 18th century, met by **Prussia, Poland, and Russia**; grain was produced on large estates by **serfs** — similar to Latin America, except that Eastern European governments (outside Poland) were much stronger.

### Visualizing the Past — West Indian Slaveholding (p. 263)

- Table traces the rise of the **plantation system on Antigua**: as sugar for export took hold, slaves and acreage concentrated in the hands of fewer, larger planters.
